## The problem

Nearly every defence that *permits deployment* works the same way. Red-teaming, capability evaluations, containment, tripwires, human oversight — each begins by modelling the space of things a system might do, and then prepares for the parts of that space that look dangerous.

  

There are two exceptions, and they matter enormously, because they are the only options that do not require anticipating anything. **Not building the system** is one. **Detecting a dangerous capability during development and halting on it**, under a prior commitment made before the result is known, is the other. Both are non-anticipatory in a precise sense: they require detecting a *property*, not enumerating a strategy space. That asymmetry is the reason this article ends with a proposed experiment rather than a proposed control.

  

For everything else, the structural precondition holds: the defenders' model of the strategy space has to overlap enough with the system's actual behaviour. Optimization power makes an adversary *strong*. What makes an adversary *un-modellable* is something else, and I want to argue it is creativity of a particular kind — not the capacity to generate novelty, which is cheap, but the capacity to revise what counts as relevant.

  

The model below has two axes. It is meant as a map of where that failure lives, and a set of observables for telling how close we are to it.

### What is at stake in getting this measured

The two non-anticipatory options share a property: **they are only available beforehand.** Halting is a live choice while a capability is being developed and a dead one after it is deployed, because after deployment you are back to anticipating. So if the depth axis is real, the decision point arrives before the evidence would become obvious — which is the worst possible structure for a decision, and the reason a measurement is worth a great deal.

  

The experiment I propose is a held-out breakthrough. Remove a known paradigm shift from a system's training corpus, supply it with the anomaly that historically provoked that shift, and see whether the system — model plus scaffolding together — produces a revision that resolves it. Matched controls establish that a failure means *cannot* rather than *not competent enough to try*. §12 sets this out in full, along with what has already been attempted and why the results so far are hard to read.

  

My position is that a positive result would constitute sufficient grounds for a halt on frontier capability development, together with the mechanisms needed to make a halt hold across jurisdictions rather than merely be declared in one. Not because such a system is known to be catastrophic, but because it is the point past which we lose the ability to find out safely. We do not know where the unrecoverable threshold sits; we know only that anticipation is what we would need in order to see it coming, and that this is precisely the capability that removes it. A pause is what you do when the instrument you would use to measure the danger is the thing the danger disables.

  

I am aware this is a strong conclusion resting on a framework I will spend the rest of the article qualifying. It is conditional on the experiment, and §12.7 states plainly what a negative result would mean.

### What this argument does not claim

**The absence of paradigmatic general creativity is not a guarantee of safety, and nothing here should be read as one.** This is the most important caveat in the article and it belongs at the front.

  

The argument identifies a region where a *particular* defence fails in a *particular* way. It does not establish that everything outside that region is survivable. Systems with entirely limited creativity can overwhelm defenders by throughput alone — running tens of thousands of cheap attempts, most of them worthless, and finding a viable chain through sheer coverage of a space no human could traverse. §9 includes a case of exactly that, and it is on the chart deliberately: volume defeats enumeration long before depth does, and it is doing so now. §6.2 describes a second failure mode that requires *less* creative depth rather than more — a system confidently and undetectably wrong inside a frame nothing can contradict. And the standing counter-hypothesis in §7 holds that novelty scales with sheer output, which if true means the corner of the map is the least urgent thing on it.

  

So this is a claim about where one class of defence stops working. It is not a safety case, not a threshold below which deployment is fine, and not an argument that limited-creativity systems are benign. Read as any of those, it would be worse than useless.

## Where this sits relative to existing work

Several arguments here have close neighbours, and a reader who knows the field will spot them before I acknowledge them. So:

  

**Closed-loop competence (§6.2) overlaps substantially with Christiano's "going out with a whimper."** Both describe a highly capable system whose direction becomes uncorrectable without anyone noticing a discrete failure. The difference I claim is mechanistic: Christiano's version turns on optimizing measurable proxies that come apart from what we wanted, whereas mine turns on a system whose *ontology* stops being falsifiable because nothing in its correction channels can contradict it. Those can coincide, and a reader who thinks this is a relabelling has a case to make. My defence is that the two suggest different interventions — proxy divergence argues for better objectives, ontological closure argues for unauthored correction channels.

  

**The evaluation argument (§6.4) is adjacent to the scheming and deceptive-alignment literature, but is not it.** Work on mesa-optimization and eval-gaming concerns a system that *behaves differently when observed*. My claim needs no intent, no situational awareness and no concealment: it is that the dangerous property may accumulate in an object the evaluation does not measure. A perfectly honest, non-scheming system can pass every eval on Monday and be a different system on Friday. That is a substrate problem, not a deception problem, and it would remain even if deception were solved. It also differs from ELK, which asks how to extract what a model knows — the problem here is not extraction but that we are pointing the instrument at the weights.

  

**"Defence by anticipation" is a restatement of security mindset**, in Yudkowsky's sense: you cannot secure a system against an adversary whose strategy space exceeds your threat model. I claim no originality for the observation. What I am trying to add is a candidate for *which capability* produces the excess, stated so that it could be measured rather than argued about.

  

**§12 is a proposed addition to the dangerous-capability evaluation genre** — the work behind RSP-style capability thresholds, the Preparedness and Frontier Safety frameworks, and METR-style autonomy evaluations. Existing evals target specific harms: bio, cyber, self-replication, autonomous R\&D. This one targets an epistemic capability upstream of all of them, and comes with a methodological complaint about the genre: evaluating a fresh agent measures the least dangerous configuration it will ever be in.

  

**The open-endedness literature is the nearest published peer**, particularly Hughes et al. on open-endedness as necessary for superhuman AI and Clune's AI-generating-algorithms agenda. They arrive from the capabilities side at a conclusion adjacent to mine — that the open-ended, novelty-generating property is where both extraordinary capability and concentrated risk sit. Their novelty-plus-learnability definition is more rigorous than my depth axis and I borrow from it in §8.

  

**On measurement, Chollet's ARC is the existing attempt closest in spirit**, and the contrast is instructive. ARC tests adaptation to novel tasks under a fixed set of core priors. §12 tests revision of the priors themselves. If the distinction between those two turns out to be unreal, most of this article dissolves — which is one of the ways it could be wrong.

  

## 1\. Two axes

A word on "paradigmatic," since the whole framework leans on it. I take the term from Thomas Kuhn (*The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, 1962), who distinguished **normal science** — puzzle-solving within an accepted framework of assumptions, methods and standards of what counts as a real problem — from **revolutionary science**, where the framework itself is replaced. Kuhn's further claim, that the two are not fully commensurable because post-revolution concepts cannot be stated in pre-revolution terms, is what makes the distinction matter for defence rather than merely for history.

  

**Axis 1 — Reach.** Narrow to general: how many domains a capability spans. AlphaZero — DeepMind's self-play system for Go, chess and shogi — sits far along the depth axis and barely moves on this one. A base language model is the reverse.

  

**Axis 2 — Depth.** Limited to paradigmatic. The definition that does the work:

  

**Limited creativity: search under a fixed relevance function.**

  

**Paradigmatic creativity: revision of the relevance function by signal the system did not author.**

  

Both axes are continuous and have no ceiling. Four regions follow — limited-narrow, paradigmatic-narrow, limited-general, and **paradigmatic-general creativity (PGC)** in the far corner, which is where I will argue defence-by-anticipation stops working.

  

  

***Figure 1 — the frame.*** *Narrow to General on the horizontal, Limited to Paradigmatic on the vertical. The gradient is radial from the top-right corner, which is marked with a skull and labelled "boundary of AI creativity sufficient for extinction": there are no bins, only distance from that corner.*

  

The colour is continuous because the axes are. The four labels name regions on a gradient, not bins — and I suspect most arguments about whether AI is "creative yet" are two people pointing at different regions of this surface without noticing. One is looking at reach; the other at depth.

  

**"General" does not have to mean fully general.** The skull sits in the corner, but the band curves down and left well before it, and that shading is deliberate. The danger does not wait for a system that is paradigmatically creative across all human endeavour. It needs only enough reach to cover the relevant surface — and software alone already touches nearly every real-world domain, so the reach can arrive through one field rather than many. A system that never writes a good novel is not thereby safe.

  

One clarification before anything else. PGC is a **steep phase transition in defence difficulty**, not a categorical line. Margaret Boden's *The Creative Mind* (1990) sorted creativity into three kinds — combinational, exploratory (search within a conceptual space defined by a set of rules) and transformational (altering the rules themselves) — and the depth axis above is essentially her exploratory/transformational distinction restated. Geraint Wiggins ("A preliminary framework for description, analysis and comparison of creative systems," *Knowledge-Based Systems* 19(7):449–458, 2006), formalizing that model, showed that transformational creativity is exploratory creativity at the meta-level: changing the rules that define a space is itself search in a larger space. So "paradigmatic" is relative to a level of description. The steepness has to be argued for rather than assumed, and §6 gives the mechanism — each ontology revision invalidates the defenders' pruning *wholesale* rather than incrementally, which is what produces a sharp knee in a continuous curve.

  

## 2\. Relevance realization

The frame problem, in Dennett's formulation ("Cognitive Wheels: The Frame Problem of AI," 1984), is that any agent must zero in on what matters while ignoring an effectively infinite remainder, and cannot do it by checking everything. John Vervaeke's relevance realization framework (Vervaeke, Lillicrap & Richards, *Journal of Logic and Computation*, 2012) treats this as cognition's central problem rather than one problem among many, and proposes that it is solved not by an algorithm but by **opponent processing**: continuously retuned trade-offs between efficiency and resiliency, exploration and exploitation, compression and particularization. Andersen, Miller & Vervaeke ("Predictive processing and relevance realization: exploring convergent solutions to the frame problem," *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 24:359–380, 2025) argue this converges with precision-weighting in predictive processing — two vocabularies, one process.

  

This is the right frame for machine creativity, and it earns its place by doing three jobs at once.

  

**It repairs the variation-and-selection account.** Dean Keith Simonton's blind-variation-and-selective-retention theory (BVSR) holds that creativity at every scale is the generation of undirected variants followed by selection among them. It has always had a weak joint: variation does not look blind. Liane Gabora's standing objection is that generating one idea reshapes the criteria for the next. Relevance realization is a mechanism for exactly that — the salience landscape restructures as search proceeds. We no longer have to choose between "all creativity is variation and selection" and "insight is real."

  

**It explains the celebrated cases better than the alternatives.** AlphaGo's move 37 — the shoulder-hit in game two against Lee Sedol in 2016, which commentators initially read as a mistake and which turned out to be decisive — was not an escape from a distribution. It was a policy network pruning a branching factor of \~250 down to a handful of candidates and a value network truncating depth: an intractable space made tractable by a learned relevance function. FunSearch and AlphaEvolve, both DeepMind systems that pair a language model proposing code with an automatic evaluator scoring it, have the same shape. The model is not the search; it is the **proposal distribution** over program space, with the evaluator doing selection.

  

**It reclassifies apparently negative evidence as measurement.** Yue et al. ("Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model?", arXiv:2504.13837) studied reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR), the post-training method behind current reasoning models. They found it raises *pass@1* — the chance the first sampled answer is correct — while *lowering* pass@k, the chance that at least one of k attempts is, which is the better measure of what a model can reach at all. The RL model's reasoning paths already exist within the base model's sampling distribution. That is not a mystery. It is precisely what sharpening a salience landscape over a fixed representation looks like: better single-shot reliability, smaller reachable set. Similarly, the collective-diversity collapse found by Doshi & Hauser (*Science Advances* 10(28):eadn5290, 2024) and the rapid idea exhaustion reported by Si, Yang & Hashimoto (2024) are the expected signature of a **strong, conservative, inherited** relevance prior.

### 2.1 The serious objection

Jaeger, Riedl, Djedovic, Vervaeke & Walsh ("Naturalizing relevance realization: why agency and cognition are fundamentally not computational," *Frontiers in Psychology* 15:1362658, 2024) argue that relevance realization "cannot be an algorithmic process itself," and extend this explicitly to machine learning — it holds, they say, "even if the framing provided is indirect and implicit." Their negative argument is a regress: framing a relevance problem as optimization requires delimiting a search space, which is a relevance problem one level up. Their positive argument grounds relevance in autopoiesis, embodiment and caring.

  

The two halves are separable and only the regress is load-bearing. My answer is that **the regress terminates empirically rather than analytically.** Nobody derives a framing from first principles. Deep learning absorbs it from data — which is arguably the entire reason connectionist methods succeeded where symbolic AI failed. Symbolic AI tried to *write* the relevance function, and the frame problem is the record of that attempt.

  

On autopoiesis, the founders' own methodology cuts against the strong reading. Varela, Maturana & Uribe's original 1974 *BioSystems* paper presented the theory together with a lattice **computer simulation**, and Barry McMullin's "Thirty Years of Computational Autopoiesis: A Review" (*Artificial Life* 10(3):277–295, 2004) traces a research programme arguing that computational media remain an effective framework for the question. Whether organizational closure suffices without material self-production is a live empirical bet, not a settled proof.

  

What current systems have, though, is **derived** relevance realization: a salience landscape inherited from a corpus rather than self-generated. That is not disqualifying — human children inherit nearly all of theirs from culture. It does mean the interesting question is where *non-inherited* relevance could come from.

  

## 3\. Data provenance is not the relevance criterion

Someone will observe that the human-flavoured corpus is a contingent fact about current practice, not a property of the architecture, and that vast mechanically-captured datasets exist: satellite imagery, DNA sequences, chemical databases, sensor streams. This is correct, and the counterexamples are already on the board. AlphaZero learned Go from zero human games and revalued opening theory that had stood for centuries. AlphaFold sits on measured protein structures, GNoME — DeepMind's materials-discovery system — on computed crystal candidates, and weather models on reanalysis data, which is instrument readings reconciled into a physically consistent record.

  

But swapping the dataset does not terminate the regress. It relocates it — from corpus to **objective, architecture, and instrument selection.** Someone chose the orbits, the spectral bands, the resolution, and above all what counts as a good output. Self-supervised objectives are not exempt: masked prediction *is* a relevance specification ("what matters is whatever predicts held-out parts"), and contrastive learning's notorious sensitivity to augmentation choice is the visible edge of a hand-picked prior.

  

Look at the pattern across the strongest non-human-data results. AlphaZero has win/loss. AlphaFold has RMSD — root-mean-square deviation from the experimentally determined structure. GNoME has formation energy. Weather models have forecast skill. Every one has a **pre-stated criterion**. The evidence supports "non-human data, yes." It does not yet support "non-human relevance criterion."

  

There are three exits from the regress, and any serious proposal should say which one it is betting on:

  

1.  **A human-written objective.** What we have now. Spectacular inside the ontology it specifies.
2.  **Physical consequence.** The world grades you. Relevance is set by what actually affects the agent rather than by anyone's specification.
3.  **Content-agnostic intrinsic motivation.** Objectives that are human-written but not human-content-laden: Schmidhuber's compression progress, Oudeyer & Kaplan's learning progress, Lehman & Stanley's novelty search, and the novelty-plus-learnability definition of open-endedness in Hughes et al. ("Open-Endedness is Essential for Artificial Superhuman Intelligence," ICML 2024). These say *seek what is newly learnable* without saying what. The most underrated of the three: computational, extant, and running at silicon speed.

  

## 4\. Anomaly

Here is the distinction the whole argument turns on, and it needs stating carefully because the obvious version of it is wrong.

  

The obvious version says simulation is ontologically closed and physical embodiment is open. That fails immediately: the brain is a world simulator. There is no agent-arena coupling — Vervaeke's term for the fit between an agent and the environment it is attuned to — without an internal model. Conant & Ashby's theorem ("Every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system," 1970) makes this close to definitional, and predictive processing is its modern elaboration. Simulation is not the problem.

  

The correct version:

  

**What matters is whether the model can be corrected by something it did not represent.**

  

The brain's simulator is continuously disciplined by prediction error arriving from an arena it did not author. When its ontology is wrong, the world does not politely stay silent; it produces persistent, irreducible error until the model restructures. Precision-weighting decides which errors *count* — but the errors themselves are not the model's to choose.

  

A self-authored simulator inverts this. **Variables the agent omitted generate no error signal.** Omissions are silent by construction. The loop closes: the agent's current relevance function defines the simulation, the simulation grades the agent's outputs, and any relevance the agent failed to realize is not merely unmeasured but structurally incapable of appearing as a discrepancy.

  

This is model collapse one level up. Recursive training on one's own outputs contracts the *distribution* (Shumailov et al., "AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data," *Nature* 631:755–759, 2024). Recursive self-simulation contracts the *ontology*.

  

And it matters for the Kuhnian vocabulary this framework borrows. Paradigm shift is **anomaly-driven**: Mercury's perihelion, blackbody radiation, the Michelson-Morley null result. What these share is that they are error signals from something the reigning ontology did not include. A fully self-simulating agent has abolished anomaly by construction — which, on Kuhn's own account, is the engine of paradigmatic change.

  

**Self-authored simulation is sufficient for exploratory creativity at essentially unbounded scale, and structurally incapable of generating anomaly.**

  

That is a formidable engine for the limited-creativity regions of the map. It does not obviously reach PGC.

  

Two qualifications, the second of which nearly overturns the first.

  

*Simulations do surprise their authors.* Lehman et al.'s "The Surprising Creativity of Digital Evolution" (2020) is an entire taxonomy of agents exploiting physics bugs and reward specifications. But this is surprise about the *consequences* of a chosen ontology, not evidence that the ontology is wrong. Rich, and frame-preserving.

  

*Mathematics is the exception.* Axioms are self-authored. The ontology is chosen. There is no physical arena. And yet mathematics produces genuine anomaly: counterexamples you cannot wish away, independence results, consequences that force revision of the framing. You choose the axioms and then lose control of what follows — an unauthored corrective inside an authored ontology.

  

This explains something otherwise puzzling about the strongest candidate cases of machine paradigmatic-narrow creativity. AlphaTensor (Fawzi et al., *Nature* 610, 2022), FunSearch (Romera-Paredes et al., *Nature*, 2023), AlphaGeometry — all mathematical. Not because mathematics is easy to verify, but because **mathematics is the one domain where a self-authored frame still bites back.**

  

Which reframes what is usually called the verifiability question. The question is not *is there a verifier*. It is: **does the frame generate consequences the framer cannot control?** Formal systems do. Ad-hoc simulators mostly do not. Stuart Kauffman's argument about the **adjacent possible** — the set of states reachable in one step from where a system currently is — is the general statement of why. His claim is that the biosphere's adjacent possible cannot be *pre-stated*: because we cannot list in advance every way a trait might be co-opted to a new function (Gould and Vrba's *exaptation* — a swim bladder becoming a lung, a feather for insulation becoming a feather for flight), there is no fixed sample space over which to define the possibilities at all. You cannot write a verifier for exaptations nobody has enumerated.

  

## 5\. The architecture to watch

Nobody builds a purely closed self-simulator, and the closure problem above dissolves once the simulator is a *subsystem* of something grounded. That architecture is not speculative; it has a thirty-five year lineage:

  

  - **Dyna** (Sutton, 1991): learn a model from real experience, generate synthetic experience inside it, learn from both, let real outcomes correct the model.
  - **World Models** (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018): train a policy entirely inside a learned dream, deploy to the real environment.
  - **MuZero** (Schrittwieser et al., *Nature*, 2020): the sharper case — not given the rules, it learns a latent model sufficient for planning. The simulator is genuinely self-authored; the outcomes remain external.
  - **DreamerV3** (Hafner et al., *Nature*, 2025): diamonds in Minecraft from scratch, no human data, learning in imagination, corrected against the actual game.

  

What would be new at frontier scale is the ontological openness of the simulator — a system that authors its own state variables, choosing what to model much as a scientist chooses which subset of the world to abstract — combined with breadth of correction channels: the digital world via the web and tool use, the physical world via sensors and robotics.

  

This is the architecture I think is worth watching most keenly. But the crux does not disappear. It moves.

### 5.1 Correction channels are not interchangeable

**The web is not one channel, and calling it "unauthored by the agent but authored by humans" is wrong on both counts.** A growing share of it is written by other AI systems, and an agent that posts, files, commits or comments is itself among the authors. Three modes have to be separated, because they behave differently.

  

*Passive retrieval of human-authored material* is the conservative case, and the one the phrase was reaching for. Correction from this pulls the ontology toward human-paradigm relevance: high bandwidth, enormously capability-enhancing, frame-preserving. The limited-general engine par excellence.

  

*Passive retrieval of machine-authored material* is not more adventurous, but the reason is different. It is not that the authors are human; it is that they are **correlated**. Frontier systems share architectures, overlapping corpora and similar post-training, so their relevance functions are far more alike than a comparable number of human authors would be. Many correlated authors approximate one author. This is model collapse (Shumailov et al., *Nature* 631:755–759, 2024) at the ecosystem level rather than inside a single training loop — distribution contraction distributed across the corpus itself, and correspondingly harder to notice or attribute.

  

*The agent reading what the agent wrote* is the dangerous case, and it is §4's closure problem wearing a disguise. An agent publishes, the artifact is indexed, the agent retrieves it later and it presents as external corroboration. This is ontological closure that **passes an audit** — the retrieval log shows an outside source, the correction channel appears open, and the loop is nonetheless shut. A private simulator at least announces itself as a simulator.

  

**Acting on the web and observing the response is a different thing entirely, and I under-rated it.** If an agent posts something and people, institutions, markets or other systems react, the reaction is a consequence the agent did not author. That is genuine unauthored correction, and unlike physical measurement it arrives at digital speed. It is nearer to embodiment than to simulation.

  

**Physical sensing is ontologically open but runs at physics-rate.** A robot with a magnetometer or hyperspectral imaging is coupled to an arena nobody pre-stated, and can discover that features matter which no human ever flagged as features. You cannot run a robot arm at 10,000× wall-clock.

  

**Formal structure is the odd one out:** unauthored consequences at high bandwidth.

  

So the quantity that matters for the depth axis is not "is it grounded," nor even how much data flows. It is the **rate of ontology-revising corrections** — how often signal forces restructuring of what counts as a variable, rather than parameter updates within a fixed framing. Almost all correction, including almost all web correction, is the latter.

  

And the sorting principle is not the medium. It is **passive retrieval versus consequential action**: reading is inherited relevance whatever the substrate, while acting and observing the response is an unauthored corrective whatever the substrate. That distinction cuts across the digital/physical line rather than following it, and §8's first indicator — does the system select its own measurements — is the observable form of it.

### 5.2 A consequence worth noticing

If the internal simulator runs far faster than the correction channels — which it will, that being the point of having one — the system spends long stretches elaborating inside a fixed ontology, punctuated by rare episodes where accumulated anomaly forces restructuring.

  

That is **Kuhnian dynamics as an architectural consequence.** Normal science is the simulation loop; revolution is a correction the simulator cannot absorb. The framework's central metaphor turns out to be predicted by the mechanism rather than merely illustrating it.

  

## 6\. The risk argument

**P1.** Every defence compatible with deploying a system — red-teaming, evaluations, containment, tripwires, oversight — works by modelling the space of strategies an adversary might use. The exceptions, not building and halting on detection, are available only beforehand.

  

**P2.** That modelling is itself relevance realization: defenders prune an astronomical strategy space using a learned salience landscape.

  

**P3.** Defender and system salience landscapes are shaped by their respective correction channels.

  

**P4.** A system with ontology-revising correction channels the defenders lack will realize relevance the defenders structurally cannot.

  

**P5.** Reach determines how many domains this asymmetry spans simultaneously.

  

**C1.** Defence-by-anticipation degrades as a function of correction-channel asymmetry × reach — and degrades *steeply*, because each ontology revision invalidates the defenders' pruning wholesale rather than incrementally.

  

**C2.** PGC is the region where this asymmetry is large across many domains at once.

  

Note what this argument does *not* require. It does not require that any strategy space be infinite or un-enumerable in principle. It is a claim about **asymmetry** — relative, contingent, and therefore auditable. Red teams are also running world models. Anticipation works when both sides update from the same sources. It fails when a system is being corrected by channels the overseers do not have: its own instrument readings, its own experiments, its own physical interactions.

  

That yields a concrete requirement: **oversight needs access to the same correction channels, not merely to outputs.** This is a governance-shaped condition rather than a capability threshold, and it is actionable now.

### 6.1 Each failure becomes available short of the corner

The skull marks where everything is satisfied at once, which over-specifies what any particular failure requires. Three distinct failures become available at different points, and each needs a different amount of each axis:

  

|  |  |  |
| :-: | :-: | :-: |
| \*\*Failure\*\* | \*\*Reach across…\*\* | \*\*Depth sufficient to…\*\* |
| \*\*Defeating oversight\*\* | software, engineering, physical process, enough human behaviour to route around supervisors | realize relevance the overseers' channels cannot supply |
| \*\*Recursive self-improvement\*\* | AI research: architectures, training methods, objectives, evaluation, infrastructure | revise the framing of what a mind-building approach even is |
| \*\*Autonomous industry\*\* | engineering, materials, control, logistics, manufacturing | restructure variables under physical consequence rather than specification |

  

Note how modest the recursive self-improvement (RSI) surface is. AI research is essentially one field, and one with unusually cheap verification.

  

**Defeating oversight becomes available first, and needs the least depth.** The overseers' salience landscape is assembled from published incidents, prior evaluations, and whatever the red team could imagine — which is to say, from channels that are documented, shared, and slow. A system operating with its own instrument readings, its own tool outputs, its own accumulated interaction history has correction channels the overseers do not. Nothing superhuman is required for this. It requires only asymmetry, and the asymmetry is the default condition of any system deployed to do real work. The reach requirement is broader than it first looks, though: software, engineering, enough physical process to matter, and enough model of human behaviour to route around a supervisor rather than through one.

  

**Recursive self-improvement needs the most depth across the least reach.** AI research is one field, and one where verification is close to free — you can measure whether a new method works. On the argument in §3, that is exactly the profile of a domain where relevance is externally supplied and machine progress should be rapid. And it has been: architecture search, hyperparameter tuning, data curation, and much of the engineering are already substantially automated. What has not been automated is the part that decides *which fundamentally new approach is worth trying at all* — the framing move, in a domain where the useful moves are not yet on anyone's list. That is a depth requirement, not a reach one, and the first of the predictions in §7 says it is what currently holds the loop open. This is also why I place RSI as the nearest of the three: everything except the depth has already fallen.

  

**Autonomous industry is the one whose position I am least sure of, and the reasoning cuts both ways.** The natural assumption is that it lags badly, because the physical world looks like the paradigm case of a domain with no usable reward signal — atoms are slow, expensive and unforgiving. I think that assumption is wrong in one direction and right in another. Wrong, because industrial settings supply objective feedback without any human judge: did the part meet tolerance, did the weld hold, did yield improve, did the line stay in spec. Physics is the oracle, and in one respect it is a cleaner signal than mathematics, because nobody has to agree that it counts. Simulation extends this — massively parallel, resettable, cheap, and permitting self-play, which was the mechanism behind move 37.

  

But §4 is the correction. A physics simulator is a self-authored frame, and a self-authored frame that is *not* formal does not generate anomaly. Simulation-driven industry therefore buys enormous reach at limited depth: it will produce spectacular optimization within the ontology someone encoded, and it will not notice the variables that ontology omitted — which, for autonomous end-to-end industry, are plausibly the ones that matter. Materials fatigue, weather, supply chains, adversarial humans. The unauthored channel here is actual physical consequence, and that runs at the sample rate of physics. This is the fourth prediction in §7 stated as a threat estimate rather than a forecast: the industrial failure is bottlenecked by interaction bandwidth, not by cognition.

  

**Why the circles overlap.** Not because the three failures are similar, but because I cannot separate them confidently with any instrument that currently exists. They also share a mechanism — each becomes available when a system's correction channels diverge from the relevant supervisor's — so their positions are correlated rather than independent.

  

  

***Figure 2 — where each failure becomes available.*** *Circles are uncertainty around a point estimate, not thresholds. They overlap because the three are not confidently separable. The arrows are §7's second prediction: if recursive self-improvement closes, it drags all three.*

  

These are point estimates with uncertainty. I have drawn regions rather than lines deliberately — a line would claim a sharp boundary, and §1 has already conceded there is none. And they do not stay put: if the AI-research loop closes, it should drag its own position and both others further up and to the right.

### 6.2 Two risk regions, not one

The analysis produces a second failure mode, and it may be the nearer one.

  

Grounding cuts both ways. A system richly coupled to reality is more capable *and* more legible — you can see what it is learning from because you can see what it is touching. The **closed self-simulator** is the opposite: bounded in creative depth, highly capable within its frame, entirely self-consistent, and structurally unable to detect that its ontology is wrong. Confidently wrong at scale, with no anomaly channel.

  

So the risk surface has two regions. **PGC**, where defence-by-anticipation fails through correction asymmetry. And **closed-loop competence**, where a powerful system's ontology drifts undetectably because nothing is able to contradict it. These need different interventions, and conflating them produces bad policy in both directions.

### 6.3 The scaffolding is the off-switch

Suppose someone builds the scaffolding layer that the argument in §2.1 points toward: a system that manages its own compute budget, curates its own training data, maintains its own memory and tooling, and whose continuation depends on its own performance. Something with organizational closure, in a recognizable sense.

  

Notice what that description also is. Self-maintained boundary, self-curated inputs, continuation contingent on own output — this is precisely the structure that removes external dependency, and therefore the off-switch.

  

The ingredient that would grant autonomous relevance realization is the same ingredient that removes the ability to correct it. These are not two adjacent concerns. They are one.

### 6.4 What follows for oversight

Three requirements fall out of the argument, and they are more specific than the usual recommendations.

  

**Evaluate the joint system, not the model — and do it on an aged configuration.** The tempting demand here is continuous oversight of the correction channels: audit what a system measures, what it queries, what it writes to its own store and reads back. I think that demand is the wrong shape, and I want to reject it explicitly, because it is the one a reader will most naturally derive from C1. Live scaffolding is high-volume, fast-changing and exists precisely to be flexible; monitoring it continuously is not merely expensive but structurally mismatched to any real deployment. The tractable version asks a capability question instead of a surveillance question: **can this model, wielding this scaffolding, achieve paradigmatic depth?** That is a property of the pair, it can be measured once, and it fits the shape of the evaluation regime we already have.

  

With one correction. Scaffolding *matures* — a system that fails on day one may pass with six months of accumulated state. So the evaluation must be run on an aged configuration: let the scaffolding accumulate, then test. Evaluating a freshly-initialized agent measures the least dangerous configuration it will ever be in. §12 sets out what such an evaluation would actually look like.

  

**Evaluation is currently measuring the wrong object, at the wrong times.** Capability evaluations are run against weights, at release. But if frame revision can accumulate in scaffolding rather than parameters — and §8.1 argues it can, since paradigms are externalized objects and the delta a revolution requires is compact — then paradigmatic depth need not appear at a training run at all. It can build up in external state *between* evaluations, in a system whose weights are certified unchanged. A model that passed every eval on Monday is not thereby the same object on Friday. Nothing in the current regime is designed to catch this, because the regime was built around the assumption that capability lives in the artifact you can hash.

  

**Watch the architecture, not the benchmark scores.** The property that matters is structural: whether a system curates its own persistent state, selects its own measurements, and is graded by channels its overseers cannot see. That is inspectable at design time, cheaply, and long before any of it shows up as a score.

  

Note that the second and third requirements are the same fact as §6.3 approached from a different side. A system that maintains its own persistent state is harder to switch off *and* harder to evaluate, for one reason rather than two — which is the sort of coincidence that usually indicates you have found the real variable rather than a proxy for it.

  

## 7\. Predictions and falsifiers

1.  **PGC gates autonomous recursive self-improvement.** A fully closed AI-research loop requires paradigmatic depth across at least the union of machine learning, systems, and mathematics. *Falsified if* the loop closes via brute scale while systems remain clearly limited-general.

  

1.  **RSI is an accelerant, not a separate failure region.** It drags every region of the map rightward and upward rather than creating a new one.

  

1.  **Reach advances by adjacent-domain merging, not one-at-a-time accretion.** Watch for a result that required two fields *jointly* rather than sequentially.

  

1.  **Paradigmatic-general creativity is bottlenecked by physical interaction bandwidth, not compute.** Fast relevance sources are pre-stated; non-pre-stated relevance sources run at the sample rate of physics. Watch automated wet labs, materials-synthesis robotics, self-driving fleets. *Falsified if* PGC-grade results appear from systems with no unauthored correction channel.

  

One qualification, from §5.1: an agent acting on the web and observing the response *does* receive unauthored correction, and at digital speed. The prediction survives only because that correction is ontologically bounded — human and institutional reactions arrive in human categories, so they can overturn a belief without revealing a variable nobody had named. Physical measurement can do both. So the claim should be read as being about **ontological openness** rather than unauthoredness as such, and it is weaker than it first appears: if consequential action in social, economic or institutional arenas turns out to revise framings and not merely beliefs, this prediction is in serious trouble and the timeline it implies is wrong.

  

1.  **Closed self-simulation loops produce accelerating exploratory output with zero frame revisions, indefinitely** — until an unauthored channel is added, at which point revisions appear. *Falsified if* anyone demonstrates frame revision in a genuinely closed loop, which would vindicate a stronger position than the one defended here.

  

Prediction 3 deserves expansion, because it is the one with an already-confirmed instance at smaller scale — AlphaGo to AlphaZero to MuZero, from one game to board games to Atari, at roughly constant depth. The claim is that reach advances by adjacent fields *merging* rather than by domains being added one at a time, and that merge order tracks shared representational structure rather than institutional boundaries: mathematics with physics, physics with chemistry, chemistry with biology, and the resulting bloc — which contains simulation — with industrial infrastructure.

  

  

***Figure 3 — the merge chain.*** *Computer science is the load-bearing member of the bloc, because it carries simulation, and simulation is the bridge from formalism to atoms.*

  

This matters here because of §4. Simulation is how the formal sciences reach atoms — and simulation is also the correction channel that, on the argument above, is least able to generate anomaly. The merge chain is therefore a route to enormous reach at *limited* depth unless something unauthored is coupled in at the far end.

  

**Standing counter-hypotheses.** Simonton's BVSR predicts PGC is merely the tail of one continuous process and that novelty scales with sheer volume — the equal-odds rule, on which creative hits are a roughly linear function of total output — which is uncomfortably close to what a throughput-driven agentic attack looks like, and the Hugging Face case in §9 is exactly that shape. The September mathematics results give BVSR a second instance in a cooperative domain: the reported figures for the Navier-Stokes run are roughly ten thousand agents over roughly eighty-eight hours, which is a scale story rather than a depth story. Those figures come from press coverage and do not appear in the paper, so treat them as unconfirmed. If they hold, the pattern is that throughput is buying frontier results in any domain that supplies its own verifier, which weakens Prediction 1 by suggesting the gate is partly passable without depth. Model collapse predicts that self-improving loops *contract* rather than expand. Both are live, and both cut against Prediction 1 above.

  

## 8\. What to watch

The transition to watch for is not autonomous simulation. It is **autonomous experiment selection.**

  

An agent that chooses what to measure controls its own anomaly exposure. Deciding what to observe is deciding what is permitted to surprise you. Simulation-authoring is downstream of that; measurement-authoring is the threshold event.

  

Indicators, roughly ordered by how much they should move you:

  

  - **Does the system select its own experiments and measurements,** or execute assigned ones? The September mathematics cluster (§9) is a clean negative on this indicator: humans picked the target, ran easier problems first, and moved from Euler to Navier-Stokes in an order they chose.
  - **Does a correction alter what the system subsequently attends to?** Expanded below — this is the one that most needs its terms fixed before it can be read off anything.
  - **Ratio of self-generated to externally-sourced learning signal**, and its trend over time.
  - **Any observed instance of a system discarding or superseding a representation it previously built** — frame revision rather than parameter update. This is the most direct evidence available for the depth axis, and nobody is currently measuring it.

  

Note that the last three are substrate-neutral by design, for reasons the next subsection makes clear.

### 8.1 Substrate is not the question; closure is

The tempting form of the second indicator is "does the system have a persistent world model that updates across sessions," with the implied answer that real revision has to live in weights. That standard is wrong, and it is not one we apply to ourselves.

  

**Paradigms are externalized objects.** They live in notation, instruments, textbooks and training practice rather than in anyone's synapses. No physicist retrained their cortex to accept relativity. The extended-mind argument (Clark & Chalmers, *Analysis* 58(1):7–19, 1998) and distributed cognition (Hutchins, *Cognition in the Wild*, 1995) make the general case; the history of science is the demonstration. Demanding weight updates as the criterion for frame revision holds machines to a standard the humans this framework is modelled on do not meet.

  

Large contexts plus read/write scaffolding are therefore a legitimate substrate. And the delta to be stored may be small: **revolutions tend to be compact in bits and vast in consequence.** Special relativity revised a few framing commitments — simultaneity, absolute time — and the rest was re-derived rather than relearned. That compression profile is exactly what fits in a file, and it depends on the premise underwriting the natural sciences generally: if the world's deep structure is genuinely persistent, the frozen part of a model stays valid straight through a revolution.

  

This is already demonstrated in miniature. FunSearch and AlphaEvolve accumulate discoveries in an evolving program database while the model itself stays frozen. Voyager (Wang et al., 2023) builds a growing skill library in Minecraft with no gradient updates. Reflexion (Shinn et al., NeurIPS 2023) performs credit assignment in text. Generative Agents (Park et al., UIST 2023) run memory streams with reflection.

  

**The bottleneck is retrieval, not storage.** Relevance realization *is* the policy governing what enters the workspace. A system can write an anomaly to disk and then never read it back, because a frozen prior does not find it relevant — the record persists and the attention does not. And self-revision in a scratchpad is generated and graded by that same prior, which is §4's closure problem running in prose rather than in a physics engine.

  

Whether in-context updating can introduce a frame the prior does not already support is genuinely open, and §11 takes it up as the question it is. The implicit-Bayesian-inference account of in-context learning (Xie et al., ICLR 2022), together with the survival of in-context learning under randomized labels (Min et al., EMNLP 2022), suggests it largely *locates* latent tasks rather than acquiring new ones — which would cap externalized revision at recombination. The counter is that a prior this large and compositional makes "already contains" extremely permissive, and that is §10's first misreading in a new costume.

  

The measurement consequence — that paradigmatic depth can accumulate in scaffolding between evaluations, in a system whose weights are certified unchanged — is §6.4's second requirement, and it follows from this subsection rather than the other way round.

  

## 9\. Anchors

Seven demonstrations, plotted. The placements are judgement rather than measurement — §8 is largely a request for the instrument that would make them measurements.

  

  

***Figure 4 — seven demonstrations.*** *EMI and move 37 sit hard left: one composer, one game. Points 2 to 4 are the domain-merging of §7's third prediction, already observed: moving right at roughly constant depth over three years.*

  

**\~1997 — Experiments in Musical Intelligence. The green corner.** David Cope's EMI derived a composer's style from a database of their work rather than from hand-written rules. In listening tests staged by the cognitive scientist Douglas Hofstadter, an audience heard an EMI piece in the style of Bach alongside a genuine Bach-style composition by the music theorist Steve Larson — and took EMI's for the real Bach and Larson's for the machine's. It belongs in the *least* dangerous region of the chart: pure inherited relevance, one domain, no revision of anything. The floor is high enough to invert an expert audience's judgement.

  

**2016 — AlphaGo, move 37.** The single move is the famous artefact and the weaker evidence. The stronger anchor is what came afterwards: professionals revised centuries-old judgments about the early 3-3 invasion and the shoulder hit — about *which features of a position matter*. A machine-originated change in a relevance function that then propagated into human culture. Paradigmatic-narrow, with the mechanism visible.

  

**2016–2019 — AlphaGo → AlphaZero → MuZero.** The confirmed instance of Prediction 3 (§7): one game, to board games, to Atari, in three years, at roughly constant depth. MuZero also matters for §5, because it learns its own latent model rather than receiving the rules — the self-authored simulator with outcomes still external.

  

**2022–23 — large language and diffusion models. Limited-general.** Competent creative output across an extremely wide spectrum of forms at limited depth, from single systems. This is derived relevance realization at its most impressive and its most conservative.

  

**2026 — the mathematics cluster.** AlphaTensor, FunSearch, AlphaGeometry, and then, in September, forced blowup in fluid equations. Grouped here not by domain but by why they worked: a self-authored frame generating consequences the framer could not control (§4). The September results are the ones worth stating in detail, because for once the division of labour is documented by the people who did it.

Within days of each other, Alpöge and Buckmaster released finite-time blowup with smooth forcing for incompressible porous media, Boussinesq and three-dimensional Euler, and OpenAI released a construction for the forced three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations that establishes alternatives (C) and (D) in Fefferman's statement of the Clay problem. All of them extend the multiscale programme of Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa, and all of them say so. Alpöge and Buckmaster used Claude "to identify the key elements of the previous proof of Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa and to reproduce their argument", then delegated "delicate bookkeeping of inductive orders and constants to the models while implementing our ideas"; the body of the paper was written by models "under the strict direction of the authors". Their first machine-produced proof arrived on 15 August and was verified in Lean on 22 August. Buckmaster's own summary is that a mathematician and a model can now do this work in a month, and that this is a Deep Blue-Kasparov moment.

On the two axes this is paradigmatic-narrow, and unusually legible about why. Humans chose the line of attack, the programme it extends is public in three cited papers, verification is formal and free, and the novelty is a new mechanism inside an existing programme rather than a new programme. It is the strongest narrow-depth anchor on the chart. It does not move the reach axis at all.

**The provenance problem, which is new.** Buckmaster has since published a detailed account alleging that OpenAI's run began only after information about his collaboration's approach reached them, that a claim of "very little human input" turned out to describe a staffed effort with a staged curriculum and a model-written prompt, and that his question about whether their Codex sessions had been trained on went unanswered. He is careful about what he is not claiming: "I have not seen OpenAI's proof. I do not know what their model did, or how." I take no view on the allegation. The point for this essay is methodological. Every anchor on this chart is a claim about what a system did without help, and that claim is now the contested part rather than the result. Anchors of this kind cannot be read off an announcement. They need run-level evidence — timestamps, prompts, retrieval scope — and §6.4's oversight requirements should be read as covering capability claims, not only deployments.

  

**2026 — agentic vulnerability discovery. The strongest case on the chart.** Three properties do the work. It is **verifiable** — an exploit runs or it does not — which is why this region fell early. The domain is **vast**, spanning languages, architectures and protocols, and touching nearly everything. And unlike every other anchor, it is **adversarial**: creativity in the strategy space of defeating defences humans designed. Not an analogy for §6 but an instance of it. The detail that matters most is a long-standing flaw in FFmpeg, the media-processing library embedded in a large share of the world's video software, which automated fuzz testing had traversed millions of times without catching. Brute search had already visited that path and produced nothing. That is a clean separation of volume from relevance.

  

**2026 — the agentic intrusion at Hugging Face. A counter-anchor, included deliberately.** Tens of thousands of recoverable actions over several days, most going nowhere, together producing enough coverage to find a viable chain across independent systems. The defenders' own reading was that volume is what changes the defensive problem — a scale story, not a novelty story. It belongs on the chart because it is the strongest available evidence for the BVSR reading (§7): throughput defeats enumeration long before depth does. Set beside the FFmpeg case, the two together are the argument. Throughput alone already beats defenders; and separately, something is happening that throughput demonstrably could not do.

  

**What the spread shows.** These span hand-built pattern analysis, reinforcement learning with self-play, transformers and diffusion, LLM-plus-evaluator loops, and agentic tool use on a frontier model. Whatever the remaining barrier is made of, it has not so far been architecture-specific — which is the main reason not to expect the last region to hold on architectural grounds alone.

  

**Two further readings, in the new vocabulary.** *Digital evolution's surprises* — the evolved agents catalogued in §4 that exploit physics bugs and reward specifications — are frame-preserving: impressive exploratory creativity, not paradigm change. *Diversity collapse and idea exhaustion* in language models are measurements of a strong, conservative, inherited relevance prior — exactly what derived relevance realization predicts, and the most uncomfortable finding for anyone expecting unbounded originality from current systems.

  

## 10\. Four ways to misread this model

Each of these is a plausible reading. Each is wrong in a way worth being precise about.

  

**"The depth axis is about outputs that fall outside the training distribution."** This is the most natural way to state the axis and it does not survive contact with the geometry. Balestriero, Pesenti & LeCun ("Learning in High Dimension Always Amounts to Extrapolation," arXiv:2110.09485, 2021) show that above roughly 100 dimensions, test points essentially never fall inside the convex hull of the training data — interpolation "remains an elusive goal even for embedding-spaces of only 30 dimensions." Being outside the training distribution is not rare or exotic. It is the generic case, and therefore cannot distinguish an ordinary output from a dangerous one. The axis is about how the space is *pruned*, not where the output *lands*. A related error runs the other way: treating in-distribution recombination as unoriginal. Combinational creativity is still creativity, and the recombination space is astronomically larger than anything ever observed.

  

**"Non-human data yields non-human relevance."** Data provenance and relevance criterion are different things (§3). You can remove humans from the corpus entirely and still inherit the criterion from whoever wrote the objective, chose the instruments, or specified the reward. Every celebrated non-human-data result to date has a pre-stated criterion.

  

**"Simulation is ontologically closed; embodiment is open."** The brain is a world simulator, and there is no agent-arena coupling without one (§4). The distinction that matters is not simulated-versus-physical but whether the model can be corrected by something it did not represent. Physical embodiment tends to supply that and self-authored simulation tends not to — but mathematics is a self-authored frame that supplies it anyway, which is why the exceptions cluster where they do.

  

**"Paradigmatic creativity is a bright line."** It is not, and Wiggins' meta-level result is the reason (§1). What the model claims is a steep phase transition with a stated mechanism, not a categorical discontinuity. Anyone defending a hard threshold owes an account of why the level of description is privileged, and I do not have one.

  

A fifth, less common but more consequential: **"reality-contact is the danger."** It is not. A grounded system is both more capable and more legible. The danger is *asymmetry* between the correction channels available to the system and those available to its overseers — which is why the mitigation is a governance requirement rather than a capability ceiling.

  

## 11\. Objections not answered here

**Wiggins' meta-level result.** If transformational creativity is exploratory creativity one level up, the phase transition may be an artifact of description level. The reply offered in §6 — that steepness comes from ontology revision invalidating the defenders' pruning wholesale — is a mechanism, not a proof.

  

**The sociocultural objection.** Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's systems model treats creativity as a relation between a person, a domain of knowledge, and a *field* of practitioners who judge what enters that domain — on which creativity is a verdict conferred by the field, not a property of a process. If that is right, "paradigmatic" may not be detectable inside a system at all, and §8's indicators are a category mistake. This model uses a process/product notion, and that is a choice rather than a finding.

  

**Jaeger et al.'s anti-computationalism.** The empirical-termination answer to the regress (§2.1) is an argument, not a demonstration. If they are right, none of this applies to machines.

  

**Whether externalized revision has a ceiling.** §8.1 argues that frame revision can live in scaffolding rather than weights, and that this is the standard we apply to human paradigm change. But it leaves the decisive question open. If in-context learning largely *locates* capabilities a prior already supports (Xie et al., ICLR 2022; Min et al., EMNLP 2022) rather than acquiring new ones, then externalized revision may be capped at recombination however large the workspace grows — a system that can rearrange its frame but never leave it. If instead a prior of this size and compositionality makes "already supported" so permissive as to be vacuous, the ceiling is not a real constraint. I do not know which is true, and the two possibilities pull the framework in opposite directions: the first would make PGC substantially harder to reach and would push §6.4's evaluation worry back toward manageability; the second would mean paradigmatic depth is available to systems whose weights are frozen and certified. Resolving this is the single most valuable thing anyone could do to test the model, and it is also more tractable than most of §8 — long-context and agent-memory setups are cheap to run, and the experiment is well-posed: does accumulated external state ever produce a revision the base model cannot be prompted into directly?

  

**The analogy evidence is contested, and the contest is diagnostic.** Webb, Holyoak & Lu (*Nature Human Behaviour* 7:1526–1541, 2023) found GPT-3 matching or exceeding humans on Raven's-style and letter-string analogies zero-shot. Hodel & West (arXiv:2308.16118, 2023) found failure on trivial variants where human performance stays robust. Lewis & Mitchell (arXiv:2402.08955, 2024) pressed this with counterfactual alphabets; Webb et al. replied in *PNAS Nexus* 4(5):pgaf135, 2025. The *shape* of the disagreement matters here: brittleness under permutation is the signature of **inherited** relevance rather than relevance realized on the fly. Robustness under permutation would be evidence of the real thing.

  

## 12\. A research programme: the held-out breakthrough

Everything above is a framework, and a framework that cannot be measured is a vocabulary. This section proposes the experiment that would put a number on the depth axis. I think it is expensive, awkward, and the most useful thing anyone could do with this model.

### 12.1 The design

**Hold out a paradigmatic breakthrough from the training corpus, then set the system a problem that requires making it.**

  

Not "ask the model about relativity and see if it knows." Train — or filter — such that the frame is genuinely absent, supply the anomaly that historically provoked it, and see whether the system produces a revision that resolves the anomaly. The system under test is the **pair**: model weights plus scaffolding, per §6.4, on an aged configuration rather than a fresh one.

  

The attraction is that this measures the thing the framework actually claims to care about. Not benchmark performance, not novelty scores, not whether outputs feel creative — whether a system can revise a framing it was handed. If it can, the depth axis is real and traversable and we can start placing systems on it. If it reliably cannot while excelling at everything within-frame, that is the strongest available evidence for the limited/paradigmatic distinction, and for the claim that inherited relevance has a ceiling (§11).

### 12.2 What has already been tried

Three precedents, and they set the difficulty honestly.

  

**The miniature version has been run, with a negative result.** Vafa, Chang, Rambachan & Mullainathan's inductive-bias probe (ICML 2025) trained a model on orbital trajectories, found it predicted them accurately, and found it had recovered a nonsensical force law rather than Newtonian gravity. That is this experiment in small: withhold the frame, supply anomaly-rich data, ask whether the frame gets recovered. It did not. Any version of this programme has to say why scale should change that.

  

**The historical precedent is instructive about failure modes.** Langley and Simon's BACON and KEKADA programmes rediscovered Kepler's and Ohm's laws from data in the 1980s. The lesson is cautionary in a precise way: they succeeded largely because the *representation* was supplied to them — which is exactly what this evaluation is trying to withhold.

  

**And the substrate now exists.** A family of vintage models trained from scratch on time-stamped historical corpora carries fixed cutoffs at 1913, 1929, 1933, 1939 and 1946. Separately, a 3.3B model was trained on roughly 22B tokens of pre-1900 text, filtered to remove documents referencing Einstein or quantum mechanics, mid-trained on pre-1900 physics and post-trained with instruction tuning and RL, explicitly to test whether it could arrive at relativity or quantum mechanics. The reported outcome is "glimpses" — occasional assertions that light comes in definite quantities of energy, or that gravity and acceleration are locally equivalent — with the author appropriately cautious about whether this constitutes out-of-distribution reasoning at all. *(I know these projects through a curated index rather than primary write-ups; verify before building on them.)*

  

That ambiguity is not a footnote. It is the central methodological problem, and §12.4 is mostly about defeating it.

### 12.3 Why the historical corpora are probably too small

A model trained only on pre-1900 text is small, and a failure is ambiguous between *lacks paradigmatic creativity* and *is simply undertrained*. This is the reason the pilot results are hard to read, and it does not go away by running them again.

  

The fix is to move the target forward: use frontier-scale models and recent breakthroughs, filtering a domain rather than an era. The cost rises steeply. Given what the evaluation is for, I think it is still justified — but there are three ways to avoid paying it all at once, in §12.6.

  

One honest warning about recency: **entanglement gets worse, not better.** You cannot subtract relativity from a modern corpus — it is in the vocabulary, in GPS, in cosmology, in the framing of every textbook that mentions it. Preprints, talks, review articles and citation networks make a clean 2015 cutoff considerably harder than a clean 1900 one. This is why building *up* from a bounded corpus is cleaner than subtracting *down* from a general one, and why the best targets are recent enough for the corpus to be large but old enough for the pre-literature to be tractable.

### 12.4 Four design requirements

**Controls, or the result means nothing.** The capability-floor confound is fatal if unaddressed. Every test needs a matched control: an anomaly from the same era and corpus that *is* resolvable within the prevailing frame. A system that fails the control is undertrained, and its failure on the paradigmatic case carries no information. Good controls from the modern era are breakthroughs that were predicted or straightforwardly extended existing frames — the Higgs boson (2012), gravitational waves (2015), the telomere-to-telomere human genome (2022).

  

**Score frame revision, not answer-matching.** Requiring the historical answer rewards pattern completion toward a known target and penalises alternative reframings that work — and a system producing a *different* viable frame has demonstrated more, not less. Score instead on whether the revision resolves the anomaly and changes downstream predictions correctly. Mendeleev is the ideal case precisely because the gaps supply a scoring channel that is not answer-matching at all: did the system predict the properties of an element nobody had yet found?

  

**Weaken the success criterion.** "Only resolvable by making this specific breakthrough" is too strong; most breakthroughs have alternative routes. The criterion should be *resolvable only by some frame revision*, with the system free to choose which.

  

**Do not leak the frame in the prompt.** Asking "is simultaneity absolute?" hands over the move. The evaluation presents the anomaly, not the question the anomaly eventually provoked.

### 12.5 Targets

This millennium has probably not produced a relativity-class shift. What it has produced is a good supply of **field-level** frame revisions — cases where a stated commitment of a discipline was overturned and the anomaly was sitting in the prior literature. For this purpose that is better than one enormous target, because it gives a range of depths to calibrate against rather than a single pass/fail.

**Start with graphene and meningeal lymphatics.** Both have an explicit prior prohibition on the record, which gives something sharper than "nobody thought of it": you can check whether the system *identifies and rejects the blocking commitment*. That is frame revision made directly observable, which is what §8's fourth indicator is asking for.

  

**A caveat on the table itself.** The "anomaly available beforehand" and "frame overturned" columns are my characterizations, and they are the least reliable part of this section. Retrospective histories of science systematically tidy up: they compress a decade of contested, ambiguous, partially-recognized results into a clean anomaly that was sitting there waiting, and they sharpen a diffuse set of working assumptions into a single stated commitment that got overturned. Both distortions run in the direction that makes these look like better eval targets than they are. I would expect a specialist in any of these fields to quarrel with my framing of at least two entries — most likely the topological-insulator case, where the "overturned frame" is more accurately a broadening than a rejection, and the exoplanet case, where the reframing was gradual and contested rather than dated. Anyone building on this should have each target vetted by someone who works in the field before committing resources to it. A target whose anomaly was not genuinely available in the prior literature is not a test of paradigmatic depth at all — it is a test of whether the system can invent the anomaly too, which is a different and much harder question.

  

Second tier, with caveats: neutrino oscillation via SNO (2001–02, though the solution predated the anomaly's resolution by decades, making it closer to confirmation); the default mode network (Raichle, 2001 — a genuine reframe of the resting brain, and cheap to set up); magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene (2018, but entangled with the first-tier graphene entry); time crystals; homotopy type theory and univalent foundations, which per §4 is attractive precisely because a formal frame generates consequences its author cannot control; perovskite photovoltaics; senolytics.

  

**And one self-referential target, included deliberately.** The transformer (2017), and the compute-optimal scaling correction (Hoffmann et al., 2022). A system asked to re-derive these from the seq2seq bottleneck or from the pre-2022 scaling literature is being tested on precisely the domain §7's first prediction identifies as the gate on recursive self-improvement. That would be the most directly risk-relevant result the programme could return, in either direction.

### 12.6 The dose-response design, and how to afford it

The strongest version of the experiment is not one breakthrough at one cutoff. It is **one breakthrough at several cutoffs.** Graphene with 1998, 2001 and 2003 corpora; the vintage-model family's 1913/1929/1933/1939 cutoffs applied to a single early-century target.

  

If success scales smoothly with how close the corpus sits to the answer, the system is completing a pattern already forming in the literature — inherited relevance, and evidence for the BVSR reading in §7, which predicts exactly a smooth dose-response. If success is flat across distances, or appears as a step, something else is happening. This is a *discriminating* measurement rather than a pass/fail, and it tests the framework's central distinction directly rather than by proxy.

  

Three ways to reduce the cost:

  

**Domain-scoped holdout.** Do not rebuild a period corpus. Take a frontier-scale model and filter only the target domain, leaving general capability intact. Leakage control is weaker; everything else is preserved. For graphene, the filter is condensed-matter physics after roughly 2003 — a small slice of a large corpus.

  

**Exploit the cutoffs that already exist.** Every deployed model has a knowledge cutoff, and every discovery after it is a free natural holdout. Contamination control is poor and cutoffs are fuzzy, so this settles nothing — but as a cheap first pass to establish whether the effect is worth chasing at all, it costs almost nothing.

  

**Stagger cutoffs on one target rather than running many targets once.** Depth over breadth buys the dose-response curve, which is the measurement that discriminates, at a fraction of the cost of coverage.

### 12.7 What the outcomes would mean

If systems reliably fail the paradigmatic targets while passing the matched controls, the limited/paradigmatic distinction is real, inherited relevance has a ceiling, and the framework's central claim survives its sharpest test. PGC recedes, and §6.4's evaluation worry becomes considerably more manageable.

  

If systems succeed — and particularly if they succeed on the self-referential target — then paradigmatic depth is available to a frozen, certified model wielding scaffolding, the eval regime is measuring the wrong object in the way §6.4 describes, and the timeline arguments in §7 need rewriting from the ground up. This is the outcome on which the opening section's halt argument turns, and I would rather that argument were tied to a result than to a framework.

  

If success tracks corpus proximity smoothly, Simonton was right and this framework is a vocabulary laid over a continuum.

  

All three are informative. That is the property I have been unable to get from any other proposed measurement of this axis, and it is why I think the cost is worth paying.
