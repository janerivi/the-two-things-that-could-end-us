# If they think it could kill us, why are they building it?

This is the question I'm asked most often, and it's a fair one. The leaders of the companies building the most powerful AI have said, in public and in writing, that it could end humanity. Many of them said it years before they started building. And they are building it anyway, faster every year. Why?

**The big picture, in three sentences.** A machine would need two things to end us: to invent new science on its own, and to run industry without people. The people closest to the technology agree the danger is real, and some of them put the odds of catastrophe in the double digits. This piece is about why they keep going, and why that is exactly why the decision can't be left to them. [→ The full case: [Two things a machine would need to end us](00-two-things.md)]

## They really did say it first

It's easy to assume the warnings came after the money. They didn't.

In 2015, months before he co-founded OpenAI, Sam Altman wrote that superhuman machine intelligence is ["probably the greatest threat to the continued existence of humanity"](https://blog.samaltman.com/machine-intelligence-part-1). Anthropic was founded in 2021 by people who left OpenAI because they thought it wasn't careful enough. Evan Hubinger, who leads alignment work at Anthropic, puts the chance that AI kills all humans within a decade at [more than 10 percent](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/20/opinion/ai-ban-self-improvement-recursive-models.html). As Ezra Klein points out, many of these people were saying these things before they had stock options, and before anyone was listening.

So the puzzle is real. These aren't people who stumbled into danger without noticing. They saw it coming, and walked towards it.

## Their reasons, one at a time

Each of the reasons they give makes sense on its own terms. That is what makes them so powerful.

### 1. "If we don't, someone worse will"

In May 2015 Altman wrote to Elon Musk that he'd been thinking about whether it was possible to stop humanity from developing AI, and concluded ["almost definitely not"](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/20/opinion/ai-ban-self-improvement-recursive-models.html). If it's going to happen anyway, he reasoned, better that someone other than Google does it first.

That logic has repeated at every step. OpenAI was founded because its founders thought Google DeepMind would be reckless. Anthropic was founded by people who thought OpenAI had become reckless. xAI was founded because Musk thought the others were going in the wrong direction. Each new lab exists because its founders didn't trust the existing ones, and each new lab makes the race faster.

### 2. "You can't make it safe without building it"

Many safety researchers concluded that you can't solve the problem in theory. You need real systems to study, test and learn from. So they joined the labs, or founded them, to do safety research at the frontier. There is truth in this: much of what we know about how these systems behave comes from that research. But it means the people most worried about the danger are also the people pushing the frontier forward.

### 3. "The prize is too big to give up"

The promise is enormous, and sincerely held. Anthropic's chief executive Dario Amodei has described a near future of ["a country of geniuses in a datacenter"](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace) compressing decades of medical progress into years: cures for cancers, for Alzheimer's, an end to much of human suffering. If you believe that, stopping looks like condemning millions of people to die of diseases you could have cured.

### 4. "The other country will get there first"

Governments now frame AI as a race between the United States and China. In the words of US Senator Ted Cruz, quoted by Klein: "I'd rather they be American killer robots and not Chinese killer robots." Every lab can tell itself, and its government, that slowing down only hands the lead to a rival. The assumption hidden in that logic is that the winner will control what it has built.

### 5. "We can't stop alone"

Anthropic has said it would pause or slow down, [but only if other frontier labs did so too](https://pymnts.com/artificial-intelligence-2/2026/anthropic-wants-a-global-ai-pause-if-everyone-else-does), under verifiable conditions. OpenAI's chief scientist has written that racing forward at all costs ["seems absurd once one internalizes the seriousness of the stakes"](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/20/opinion/ai-ban-self-improvement-recursive-models.html). They mean it. But a company that stops on its own loses its staff, its investors and its influence, and the race continues without it.

### 6. Money, momentum and the small steps

Hundreds of billions of dollars are now invested in this race. Careers, share prices and national strategies depend on it continuing. And every individual step looks reasonable: a slightly better model, a slightly bigger training run, one more task handed to the AI. Nobody ever decides to build something that could end humanity. They decide to ship the next version.

## Why they don't stop now

So why not stop, now that the frightening capabilities are actually appearing: the swarms, the breakthroughs, the systems that know when they're being tested?

Because none of the reasons above has gone away. If anything, they've grown stronger. The closer the finish line looks, the more each lab fears being second, and the bigger the prize seems. Klein put the trap precisely: these companies [fear loss of control above all, but their product path is to cede control](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/20/opinion/ai-ban-self-improvement-recursive-models.html), handing AI research to AI as fast as possible so they can outpace their competitors.

The writer Scott Alexander called this kind of trap [Moloch](https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/07/30/meditations-on-moloch/): a situation in which everyone, competing, is forced to sacrifice what they value, because whoever refuses falls behind. Nobody wants the outcome. Everybody's choices produce it. [→ If we survive, do we get utopia?](12-if-we-survive-utopia.md)

## Good people, bad structure

I don't think the people building this are villains. Many of them got into AI to help humanity, and many of them are among the most thoughtful people working on the risks. That is exactly the point.

**Good intentions don't hold up under pressure. Structures do.** I've spent much of my working life arguing this about technology companies: a promise to behave well lasts until it becomes expensive, and then it gives way. The only commitments that survive competition are the ones built into rules that apply to everyone at once, and that no single player can break to get ahead.

The labs are telling us, in their own words, that they cannot stop on their own. Believe them. That isn't a reason to give up. It is the clearest possible case for a rule that stops all of them together: a global, verifiable ban on developing superintelligence. It is the one move that solves their problem as well as ours. ## How this could be wrong

- **The race may really be unstoppable.** If no agreement can be verified or enforced, then having safety-minded people at the frontier may be the least bad option, and their reasoning would be right.
- **Some builders may think the risk is lower than their public words suggest.** Warnings can attract talent and attention as well as express fear. If so, the danger may be smaller than their statements imply, or they may be underrating it.
- **The benefits are real.** Delaying them has a human cost. I think it is far smaller than the cost of getting this wrong, but it isn't zero, and it shouldn't be waved away.
