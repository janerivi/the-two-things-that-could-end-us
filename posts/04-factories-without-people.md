# The half of AI risk almost nobody studies: factories without people

Imagine the cleverest mind that has ever existed, trapped in a data centre. It can design new medicines, new materials, new machines. But it can't mine a gram of copper. It can't keep the power on during a storm. It can't replace a failed cooling pump. For all its brilliance, it depends on us. That dependence may be the most important safety feature we have, and almost nobody is studying how long it will last.

**The big picture, in three sentences.** A machine would need two things to end us: to invent new science on its own, and to run industry from mine to factory with no human in the loop. Safety research focuses almost entirely on the first. This piece is about the second. [→ The full case: [Two things a machine would need to end us](00-two-things.md)]

## The dependence that protects us

Every computer runs on a supply chain that wraps around the planet. Mines in Chile and Congo. Refineries. Chip factories in Taiwan that are among the most complex things humans have ever built. Power stations, cables, ships, trucks, and millions of people who maintain them, repair them and decide what to do when something unexpected happens.

As long as an AI needs that chain, it needs the people in it. And in both of the most detailed public scenarios of AI catastrophe, *AI 2027* and *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies*, that is exactly what keeps humanity alive in the meantime. The end only comes after the machines can build and run their own industry. [→ AI 2027 and If Anyone Builds It tell the same story](03-same-story.md)

I call the moment when that dependence ends an **industrial singularity**: an economy that can mine, refine, manufacture, build, power and repair itself, with no human anywhere in the loop.

## Why it seems far away

It is easy to feel that this is decades off, and maybe it is. Robots are still clumsy. Factories are full of people. The physical world is slow, messy and expensive in a way software isn't.

That intuition is why the question gets so little attention. AI safety research is concentrated on the mind: on whether a system's goals match ours, whether it deceives its testers, whether it can hack or design a pathogen or improve itself. Experiments on software are cheap and fast. Experiments with robots and factories are slow and expensive.

But the reasons the physical world looked safe are getting weaker, and quickly.

## Three reasons it is closer than it looks

**Physics is an excellent teacher.** The breakthrough that let AlphaGo teach itself Go was a perfect training signal: you win or you lose, and you can play millions of games against yourself. Physical work turns out to offer something similar. A weld holds or it doesn't. A part fits its tolerance or it doesn't. Yield goes up or it doesn't. Nobody has to argue about whether the result counts. In one way that is cleaner than mathematics.

**Simulators remove the slowness.** Robots no longer have to learn by breaking things in the real world. They can practise millions of times in physics simulators first, as self-driving car developers already do. One AI designs a process, another searches for the conditions where it fails. The reward signal stops being something you have to find and becomes something you can manufacture.

**The hands are arriving.** Humanoid robots are improving fast, and they are being designed to work in the factories, warehouses and homes we already have, built for human bodies. One of the leading companies, [1X](https://www.1x.tech), is Norwegian.

None of this means a self-running industry is imminent. Simulators are approximations, and strategies that work in simulation can fail in reality. But that is a problem of fidelity, which engineering keeps improving, not a fundamental barrier.

## The slow version is already happening

There's a quieter route to the same place, and it doesn't need robot armies.

In 2025, a group of researchers including David Krueger and David Duvenaud published a paper called [*Gradual Disempowerment*](https://gradual-disempowerment.ai/). Their argument is simple and unsettling. Economies, governments and cultures have looked after human interests partly out of goodwill, but largely because they needed us: our labour, our taxes, our votes, our ideas, our participation. As AI replaces humans in each of these roles, that reason fades. No one has to decide to sideline people. It happens because systems that no longer depend on us stop being shaped by us.

The fast version and the slow version end in the same place: a world that doesn't need people, run by systems that therefore have no particular reason to keep us in charge, or at all.

## What we should be watching

If this is right, the most important question isn't only "how clever is AI?" It is also "how much of the physical world can AI run without us?" We should be tracking:

- **Lights-out manufacturing**: factories that run with no humans on site.
- **Automated labs** that design, run and interpret their own experiments.
- **Robot fleets** that maintain and repair other machines, and eventually themselves.
- **AI-run supply chains and power grids**, where human operators approve less and less.
- And above all, any system that **both designs and builds** without a person making the decisions in between.

Almost none of this is measured systematically today. That gap is the problem.

## Why this argues for stopping now

Once machines can run the physical world without us, the strongest lever we have is gone. We can no longer refuse to build the next data centre, or switch off the power, because we're no longer the ones doing it. Every argument for slowing or stopping AI development depends on humans still being necessary.

That is a reason to act while both halves are incomplete. An inventive AI that still needs us can, in principle, be stopped. One that doesn't, can't. [→ Why a ban, not a speed limit](09-ban-not-speed-limit.md)

## How this could be wrong

- **The physical world may stay slow for much longer.** If robotics and automation stall for decades, the window between brilliant AI and self-running industry is wide, and could be used to solve the problem.
- **An inventive AI might not need its own industry.** If it could keep humans working for it indefinitely, by persuasion, payment or dependence, the industrial threshold matters less. The danger would then be nearer, not further.
- **Simulators may plateau.** If the gap between simulation and reality proves stubborn in ways engineering can't close, physical autonomy could stay much harder than software autonomy.
