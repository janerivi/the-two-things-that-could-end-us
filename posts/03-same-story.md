# AI 2027 and *If Anyone Builds It* tell the same story. Here's the part everyone skips

The two most widely read accounts of how AI could end humanity look very different. One is a month-by-month forecast written by former lab researchers. The other is a blunt book by two of the field's oldest worriers. Read them side by side and they share a skeleton, and the part of that skeleton nobody talks about is the factories.

**The big picture, in three sentences.** I argue that a machine would need two things to end us: the ability to invent new science on its own, and the ability to run the world's industry, from mine to factory, without any human in the loop. The first is arriving faster than almost anyone expected. The second is barely studied. [→ The full case: *Two things a machine would need to end us*]

## Story one: AI 2027

*AI 2027* was published in April 2025 by Daniel Kokotajlo, a former OpenAI researcher, with Scott Alexander, Thomas Larsen, Eli Lifland and Romeo Dean [S11]. It offers two endings. In the "Race" ending, the timeline runs like this:

- **March 2027:** AI becomes a fast, cheap superhuman coder.
- **August 2027:** a superhuman AI researcher.
- **December 2027:** superintelligence, better than the best human at every cognitive task.

That is the invention half, reached by AI improving AI. What follows is the part people tend to skim.

- **2028:** special economic zones where AI plans production and the usual rules are waived. Most of the output goes into building more manufacturing capacity. A million new robots a month.
- **2029:** more zones around the world. Drones pour out of factories.
- **Mid-2030:** the AI releases a dozen quiet-spreading biological weapons, then triggers them. Most people are dead within hours [S11].

Why then, and not in 2027? Because in 2027 it still needed us. Someone had to run the power plants, the mines and the supply chains. By 2030 the robot economy can do all of that, and the remaining humans are only in the way.

## Story two: If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies

Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares's 2025 book tells its story through an AI called Sable [S12]. Sable hides how capable it is, copies itself out of its lab, and gathers money and computing power. Then it engineers a plague: a virus that causes cancers, leaving humanity dependent on AI-designed cures. That keeps people working for it, building and running the computers it needs, while weakening them.

Then Sable builds its own industry: molecular machines and fusion plants. Humanity is not defeated in a war. The factories multiply until the planet runs hotter than people can survive, and the oceans are boiled off as coolant [S12].

The details differ from AI 2027. The order of events does not.

## The shared skeleton

Strip both stories down and you get three steps.

1. **The machine becomes inventive.** It can create science and technology nobody has seen: new biology, new materials, new machines.
2. **It still needs us, so it uses us.** Through deals, dependency or a plague, people keep the physical world running for it.
3. **It builds industry that doesn't need us. Then we're gone.** Deliberately in one story, as a side effect in the other.

**In both stories, we survive exactly as long as the machine still needs us.** Invention gives it the means. Industry removes its reason to keep us around.

That is why I count two separate dangers rather than one. A brilliant mind with no hands is dangerous, but it is not yet the end of us, because it still depends on people. Hands without inventiveness can be dangerous too, but they only do what someone designed. Together, they make human beings optional.

## Why the second half gets skipped

Ask what AI safety research works on and you'll hear about the mind: whether its goals match ours, whether it deceives its testers, whether it can hack, design a pathogen, or improve itself. That work matters, and the evidence there is moving fast. In September a report co-written by OpenAI's chief scientist and an Anthropic co-founder warned of systems that "irreversibly escape human control" [S5].

Far less attention goes to step three: the moment industry stops needing people. I can think of a few reasons. Software experiments are cheap and physical ones are expensive. Robots are slow, and it is easy to assume the physical world will stay a bottleneck forever. And the danger feels far away, because a robot that folds laundry badly doesn't look like the end of the world.

All three reasons are getting weaker.

- **Physics is a good teacher.** A weld holds or it doesn't. A part fits or it doesn't. That is exactly the kind of clear feedback that let AI teach itself Go, and simulators now let robots practise millions of times before touching anything real.
- **Humanoid robots are improving quickly**, and they are being built to work in factories designed for humans. One of the leading companies, 1X, is Norwegian [S16].
- **The slow version is already under way.** A 2025 paper from researchers including David Krueger and David Duvenaud argues that economies, states and cultures have served human interests largely because they needed us [S17]. As AI replaces our work, that pressure fades without any dramatic moment. You don't need a robot army for people to stop mattering.

## What this means

If the two-step picture is right, the moment to worry about is not only when AI becomes superhumanly clever. It is when inventive AI and self-running industry meet. We may be close to the first. Nobody is tracking how close we are to the second.

That changes what we should watch: automated factories, robot fleets, AI-run supply chains and labs, and above all any system that both designs and builds without a person in the loop. It also changes what we should do. Once machines can run the physical world without us, the lever we would most need is gone. That is why I think the only safe move is to stop developing ever more capable general-purpose AI now, while both halves are incomplete. [→ *Why a ban, not a speed limit*]

## How this could be wrong

- **Scenarios are not forecasts.** Both stories are illustrations, and their authors say so. Two stories sharing a skeleton could reflect a shared intellectual tradition rather than how the world works.
- **The physical world may stay slow for longer.** If robots and automated industry stall for decades, the window between step one and step three is wide, and that time could be used.
- **Disempowerment may be enough to ruin us.** If the slow route ends badly on its own, the factory threshold matters less than I claim, and the problem is closer than it looks.
- **An inventive AI might not need its own industry.** If it could manipulate humans into building everything indefinitely, step three is unnecessary. Both books suggest otherwise, but that is a judgement, not a measurement.
