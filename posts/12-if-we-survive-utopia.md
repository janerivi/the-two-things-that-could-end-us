# If we survive, do we get utopia?

Suppose the worst doesn't happen. The machines don't turn on us; nobody releases a plague; the planet doesn't overheat. Does that mean we get the future the AI companies advertise: cures, abundance, freedom from drudgery? Not necessarily. There are futures in which we survive, comfortable and fed, and no longer matter.

**The big picture, in three sentences.** A machine would need two things to end us: to invent new science on its own, and to run industry without people. This series mostly asks what happens if both arrive and the machine doesn't want us around. This piece asks what happens if they arrive and we're simply no longer needed. ## Three ways to survive badly

### 1. Gradual disempowerment

In 2025, researchers including David Krueger and David Duvenaud published a paper called [*Gradual Disempowerment*](https://gradual-disempowerment.ai/). Their argument is that economies, states and cultures have served human interests largely because they *needed* us. Companies needed workers and customers. Governments needed taxpayers, soldiers and voters. Culture needed people to create it.

As AI replaces humans in each role, that need disappears. No single decision sidelines people. It happens through thousands of reasonable ones: automate this department, let the system draft that policy, recommend what people see. Each system that no longer depends on us is shaped a little less by us. The authors' conclusion is sobering: there is, as yet, no concrete plan for stopping it.

### 2. Power in very few hands

The September report on automating AI research, co-written by Hinton, Bengio and senior scientists at OpenAI, Microsoft and Anthropic, warns of a different danger: that an intelligence explosion could [turn a small lead into a decisive one](https://casp.ac/reports/intelligence-explosion), and "severely erode checks and balances within and between states, companies, and branches of government".

Every system of government we have relies on power being spread out, and on the powerful needing the cooperation of many others. A government that no longer needs soldiers or officials, or a company that no longer needs employees, faces fewer limits than any in history. Even with the best intentions, that is not a safe arrangement. With ordinary human intentions, it is a dangerous one.

### 3. The race to the bottom

The writer Scott Alexander gave this one a name: [Moloch](https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/07/30/meditations-on-moloch/), after the ancient god to whom children were sacrificed. Moloch is what happens when everyone, competing, is forced to sacrifice what they value in order not to lose. No one wants it; everyone does it, because whoever refuses falls behind.

Ezra Klein describes the AI race in exactly these terms: [a tragic collective action problem](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/20/opinion/ai-ban-self-improvement-recursive-models.html), in which each company and country fears that the others care less about safety, so all of them race. In a world of powerful AI, the same pressure applies to everything else. The firm that keeps human workers loses to the one that doesn't. The country that keeps humans in charge of decisions loses to the one that lets machines decide. Nobody chooses a world without human agency. It is chosen for us, one competitive step at a time.

Two short films from the Omeleto channel, [*MOLOCH*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q4aUidMZxbs) and [*DELISTED*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LpQXTn7g_AA), explore what futures like these could feel like from the inside.

## Why "they'll keep us comfortable" isn't reassuring

A common answer is that even in these futures people will be well looked after: universal basic income, cheap goods, medical miracles. Maybe. But comfort granted by systems that don't need you is comfort that can be withdrawn. The historical protection of ordinary people has never been the kindness of the powerful. It has been that the powerful needed them.

And a future in which people are fed and entertained but have no say in where their world is going is not one most of us would choose, if we were asked. The point is that we might not be asked.

## The connection to the two capabilities

These futures and the extinction scenarios share a root. In each, the decisive change is that **humans stop being necessary**: to research, to industry, to government, to culture. Invention and self-running industry are what make people unnecessary. Whether the result is extinction or disempowerment depends on what the systems, and whoever controls them, then choose. That is not something we should leave to chance, or to a handful of companies. [→ Factories without people](04-factories-without-people.md)

## What follows

This is another reason why the right response is to stop pushing the frontier now, rather than hoping to steer a world in which humans are already optional. Decisions about what kind of future we want are only ours to make while we still have the leverage that comes from being needed. [→ Why a ban, not a speed limit](09-ban-not-speed-limit.md)

## How this could be wrong

- **Societies may adapt.** New institutions might keep power spread out and people in charge, even when their labour isn't needed. Democracy has adapted to huge economic changes before.
- **AI might strengthen us rather than replace us.** Tools that make every person far more capable could spread power out instead of concentrating it. That is the optimistic case, and it is worth fighting for. But it requires choices we are not currently making.
- **The pressures may be weaker than they look.** Markets and states are not perfectly competitive, and people can refuse to race. That refusal is what this series is asking for.
