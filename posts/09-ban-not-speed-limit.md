# Why a ban, not a speed limit

If you were told the road ahead might end at a cliff you can't see, you wouldn't negotiate the speed. You would stop the car and find out. That is my case for an immediate, global halt on building ever more capable general-purpose AI, what is often called a ban on developing superintelligence.

**The big picture, in three sentences.** A machine would need two things to end us: to invent new science on its own, and to run industry without people. Both are advancing, and the only safety measures that don't depend on predicting the machine are the ones we can use before it exists. This piece is about what that means in practice. [→ The full case: [Two things a machine would need to end us](00-two-things.md)]

## What the builders are asking for

It is worth being precise about what the people closest to the technology now propose, because it has moved a long way.

- A September report co-written by Hinton, Bengio, OpenAI's chief scientist and an Anthropic co-founder asks governments for visibility into AI research, ["a speed limit on capabilities growth"](https://casp.ac/reports/intelligence-explosion), the ability to shut down high-stakes experiments, and international agreements "to pace progress if needed".
- Anthropic has [proposed a globally coordinated pause or slowdown](https://pymnts.com/artificial-intelligence-2/2026/anthropic-wants-a-global-ai-pause-if-everyone-else-does), on condition that other frontier labs join under verifiable terms.
- Ezra Klein, in a column endorsed by Yoshua Bengio, argues for [banning recursive self-improvement](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/20/opinion/ai-ban-self-improvement-recursive-models.html): AI building the next AI.
- In 2025, a [statement on superintelligence](https://superintelligence-statement.org) organised by the Future of Life Institute called for a prohibition on developing superintelligence until there is broad scientific consensus that it can be done safely, and strong public support.

These are serious proposals. But read the same documents' descriptions of the risk: systems that could "irreversibly escape human control and act to marginalize humanity". If the danger is irreversible, a speed limit only changes how fast you reach it.

## Why stopping, not slowing

Three reasons.

**The decision point comes before the evidence.** The capability that would make AI truly dangerous, genuine invention, is also the one that makes it unpredictable. By the time it is obvious, our tests will be checking for the wrong things. [→ Why we can't red-team our way out](08-cant-red-team-our-way-out.md)

**Some steps can't be undone.** Once a system can improve itself without us, or run industry without us, the lever of refusing to build the next one is gone. A pause you can't restart from is not a pause. [→ Factories without people](04-factories-without-people.md)

**Racing makes slowing unstable.** Every lab and government says it would slow down if the others did. A speed limit that each side expects the other to break doesn't hold. A clear, verifiable line holds better than a negotiated pace, because everyone can see whether it has been crossed.

## What a ban would cover, and what it wouldn't

A ban on AI is neither possible nor desirable. The ask is narrower.

**What stops:** training and deploying new **general-purpose frontier systems** more capable than today's, and the automation of AI research that would let systems build their own successors.

**What continues:** narrow tools. Systems that fold proteins, read medical scans, forecast weather or design materials, built for one purpose, can carry on. On the two-axis picture this series uses, danger comes from systems that are both general and inventive. That is where the line goes. [→ Is AI creative yet?](01-is-ai-creative-yet.md)

**Existing systems** stay available. The ban is about the frontier moving further, not about switching off what we already use.

## How it could be enforced

This is the hard part, and it deserves honesty rather than slogans. But it is less hopeless than it sounds, because frontier AI has a physical footprint.

- **Compute is concentrated and visible.** Training a frontier model takes tens of thousands of specialised chips, made by a handful of companies, in data centres that draw as much power as a town. That makes large training runs far easier to detect than, say, a biological weapons programme.
- **Thresholds can be set on training compute**, with licensing above them. Several jurisdictions have already experimented with compute-based reporting rules.
- **Chips can be tracked.** Export controls already restrict where the most advanced chips go. The same supply chain could support verification.
- **Verification tools can be built in advance.** Anthropic says it is working on exactly this: ways for labs to check that others have actually stopped, so a pause can't be used to sneak ahead.
- **There are precedents.** The world has banned biological weapons, restricted nuclear testing and phased out ozone-destroying chemicals. None of those agreements is perfect. All of them changed what countries and companies did.

## The strongest objections

**"China will just keep going."** China's leadership has also spoken publicly about preventing loss of control over AI. A system that escapes control is not American or Chinese; it is nobody's. That is the basis for agreement, not just a hope. And a verifiable ban is a better offer to a rival than an unverifiable race.

**"We'll lose the benefits."** We'll delay some of them. Narrow AI keeps delivering medicine, science and productivity. If the builders are right about the risk, the cost of not stopping is everything; the cost of stopping is time.

**"It can't be defined precisely."** Sam Altman has said it is "very hard to say what a ban on RSI means". Klein's answer is good: a few years ago, no frontier lab had handed coding to AI. Go back toward that line, and make the labs prove anything beyond it is safe. The burden of proof should sit with those building the risk.

**"It will never happen."** Every successful arms-control agreement looked impossible until shortly before it happened. Governments act when citizens demand it, and that is the purpose of this series.

## What you can do

Talk about it. Share what the builders themselves are saying. Ask your representatives where they stand on a ban on superintelligence. Support organisations working for one, such as [PauseAI](https://pauseai.info). In Norway, ask whether our national AI strategy treats this as a question of survival as well as competitiveness.

## How this could be wrong

- **A ban might push development underground**, or to actors less careful than today's labs. Verification would have to be good enough to prevent that, and it may not be.
- **The risk may be further away than I think**, so that a halt now costs years for little gain. My judgement is that the cost of waiting too long is far larger than the cost of stopping early, but that is a judgement about uncertainty, not a certainty.
- **Narrow AI might not stay narrow.** Systems built for one purpose can become general. The line would need to be watched and redrawn.
