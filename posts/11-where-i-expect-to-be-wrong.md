# Where I expect to be wrong

I'm asking for something drastic: a global halt on the most powerful technology humans have ever built. An argument for that should be attacked hard, and the person making it should help. So here are the places I think my case is weakest, and what would change my mind.

**The big picture, in three sentences.** I argue that a machine would need two things to end us: to invent new science on its own, and to run industry without people. Both are advancing, and the only defences that don't depend on predicting the machine are the ones we use before it exists. Below are the ways that argument could fail. [→ The full case: [Two things a machine would need to end us](00-two-things.md)]

## 1. "Invention" might not be a real threshold

My whole picture rests on a difference between being clever inside a framework and changing the framework. The cognitive scientist Geraint Wiggins showed that changing the rules can itself be described as searching a bigger space of possible rules. If he's right in the way that matters, there's no steep cliff, just a smooth slope, and the danger grows gradually enough to track.

**What would show it:** an experiment where AI's ability to rediscover hidden breakthroughs rises smoothly as it is given more and more of the surrounding knowledge. [→ An experiment that would tell us](10-experiment-safe-to-resume.md)

## 2. Volume might do what depth does

The psychologist Dean Keith Simonton argued that creative breakthroughs, in humans, come roughly in proportion to how much someone produces. Most attempts fail; enough attempts produce a hit. If that holds for machines, then you don't need genuine invention at all, just enough attempts in a field that can check the answers.

That cuts both ways. It would mean my "corner of the map" matters less than I claim. But it would also mean the danger is nearer, because volume is exactly what AI has in abundance. The Hugging Face swarm and the huge maths runs both look partly like volume stories.

## 3. Machines might never get there at all

Some researchers, including collaborators of John Vervaeke, whose ideas I lean on, argue that the ability to sense what matters can't be produced by any computation. It depends on being a living, self-maintaining thing that cares about its own survival.

If they're right, AI will plateau below genuine invention, and much of this series worries about something that can't happen. I think the success of modern AI, which learned relevance from data after decades of failing to program it by hand, is evidence against them. But it isn't proof.

## 4. The physical world might stay slow for decades

My second capability, industry without people, might be much further off than the first. Robots are still clumsy and simulators still approximate. If self-running industry stays decades away, there is a long window in which an inventive AI still depends on us, and that window could be used to solve the problem rather than stopping. [→ Factories without people](04-factories-without-people.md)

## 5. The scenarios might be illustrations, not forecasts

I lean on *AI 2027* and *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies* to show why both capabilities matter. Their authors say themselves that they are illustrations. Two stories sharing a skeleton might reflect a shared intellectual tradition rather than the shape of reality. [→ AI 2027 and If Anyone Builds It tell the same story](03-same-story.md)

## 6. Some of my strongest numbers come from the companies

"26 percent of research tasks." "More than 80 percent of code." "An automated research intern." These figures come from Anthropic and OpenAI, which have commercial reasons to show fast progress. I have marked them as company figures, but the pattern of my argument depends on them more than I would like.

There is a further conflict I should name. I used an Anthropic model to help research and draft this series, and the same model tops one of the measurements I cite. I have tried to check everything against primary sources, but you should know.

## 7. A ban might backfire

A halt that isn't verifiable might push development underground, or towards less careful actors. If verification can't be made good enough, a ban could make things worse. I think frontier AI's enormous physical footprint makes verification feasible, but that is an engineering bet. [→ Why a ban, not a speed limit](09-ban-not-speed-limit.md)

## 8. I changed my mind, and that should cost me something

A month ago I argued that a halt should wait for a clear experimental result. Now I argue for one immediately. My reason is internal to my own argument: the moment to stop arrives before the evidence becomes obvious, and the evidence this autumn moved almost every indicator I track. But a reader is entitled to ask whether I am reacting to a dramatic season of news rather than to a change in the underlying picture.

## What I don't think I'm wrong about

That the people building these systems say they could cause human extinction, and keep building them. That every safety method we rely on assumes we can predict what the system will do. And that the costs of being wrong are wildly asymmetric: if I'm wrong and we stop, we lose years; if the builders are right and we don't, we may lose everything.

If you think I'm wrong somewhere not listed here, I want to hear it.
