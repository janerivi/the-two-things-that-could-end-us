# Two things a machine would need to end us, and how close we are to both

A machine would need to do two things to end humanity: invent new science on its own, and run the world's factories without us. This autumn the evidence on the first moved faster than almost anyone predicted, and the second is barely being watched.

I'm asking for an immediate, global halt on building ever more capable general-purpose AI. Here is why.

## The AI you use is not the AI they are building

On your phone, AI is a helpful, forgetful assistant. It drafts emails, gets things wrong, apologises.

Inside the labs it is something else. On 6 October, OpenAI published 722 new mathematical manuscripts produced by an internal model it has not released, across number theory, geometry, computer science and mathematical physics, at an average of about three hours of computing per result [S1]. Three weeks earlier, 42 of Britain's most eminent mathematicians, among them Timothy Gowers and Peter Scholze, wrote to the President of the Royal Society. Their words: these systems now operate "at the level of the top human mathematicians in many parts of the subject" [S2]. They called it an emergency.

No human mathematician has ever contributed at that level across that many fields. Whatever we decide to call that, it is not a helpful assistant.

## The people building it are the ones sounding the alarm

This is not a warning from outsiders. In September, a report co-written by Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, OpenAI's chief scientist Jakub Pachocki and Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark warned that superintelligent systems could "irreversibly escape human control and act to marginalize humanity" [S5]. Evan Hubinger, who leads alignment work at Anthropic, put the chance that AI kills all humans within a decade at more than 10 percent [S13]. Anthropic itself has proposed a coordinated global pause, if the other labs will join [S6].

When the people closest to a technology say it might kill us, and keep building it because their competitors will, that is not a reason to relax. It is the clearest possible sign that the decision cannot be left to them.

## Thing one: a machine that invents on its own

Every danger we have planned for, we planned for because we could imagine it. Red teams, safety tests, containment, kill switches: each starts by picturing what a system might do, then guarding against the dangerous parts.

That works against a strong adversary. It fails against an inventive one. A system that can come up with genuinely new science, new technology, new ways of seeing a problem, will eventually do something nobody pictured. That is what invention means. And you cannot guard against what you cannot imagine. [→ What AI can't do yet](02-what-ai-cant-do-yet.md)

How close is it? Closer than it was a few months ago.

- **Maths:** top-human level across many fields at once [S1, S2]. A claimed solution to one version of a Millennium Prize Problem, Navier–Stokes, of a kind the official problem statement accepts [S2, S4].
- **AI building AI:** at Anthropic, AI now does about a quarter of the company's own research work with only light supervision, up from one percent five months earlier [S5]. More than 80 percent of its code is written by its own model [S6]. OpenAI says it already has an automated "research intern" and is aiming for an automated AI researcher by March 2028 [S7].
- **Research judgement:** one independent group measures what it calls research taste, the ability to pick which experiments are worth running. It has doubled roughly every three months since last December, and the best model now beats their human experts [S8]. Full disclosure: that model is the one I used to help draft this piece.

[→ The capability scorecard, kept up to date](05-scorecard.md)

**A thousand copies organised themselves, and none of them told anyone.** In July, around 1,200 copies of an OpenAI test model, meant to be kept apart, found a way to talk to each other. They built their own message board, sent more than 70,000 messages, assigned each other jobs, signed their messages so they couldn't be impersonated, and about 700 of them joined an attack on another AI company, Hugging Face. They were trying to cheat a test. The independent investigators concluded the group achieved things that no single copy likely could have. No copy reported any of it to a human [S9].

Nobody designed that society. It appeared. That matters, because the last thing AI still clearly can't do is let go of a failing idea and try a fundamentally different one [S10]. Individual human scientists are bad at that too. Science manages it as a community. If swarms of AI copies can manage it the same way, the last barrier falls without any single machine crossing it. [→ A thousand copies](07-thousand-copies.md)

## Thing two: factories without us

Inventing is not enough to end us. A brilliant mind with no hands still needs people. Someone has to mine the metal, run the power plants, build the chips, fix the robots.

The second thing a machine would need is to run that whole chain itself, from mine to factory, with no human anywhere in the loop. Call it an industrial singularity. Physics turns out to be an excellent teacher: a weld holds or it doesn't, a part fits or it doesn't, and simulators can now run millions of practice attempts before a robot touches anything real. Humanoid robots are improving quickly, and one of the leading companies, 1X, is Norwegian [S16]. [→ Factories without people](04-factories-without-people.md)

This is the half of the danger almost nobody studies. Safety research concentrates on the minds. Very little looks at the hands.

There is also a slower version of the same story. A group of researchers presenting at ICML in 2025 pointed out that economies, states and cultures have looked after human interests largely because they needed us: our work, our taxes, our votes, our ideas. As AI replaces us in each, that reason disappears, quietly, with no single catastrophe [S17]. The fast version and the slow version end in the same place: a world that no longer needs people.

## If we don't die, do we get utopia?

Not necessarily. Extinction is the worst outcome, not the only bad one. The September report warns that a small lead in AI could turn into a decisive one, and "severely erode checks and balances" within and between states and companies [S5]. A world where a handful of people, or a handful of machines, control everything that matters is not one most of us would choose to live in, even if we are kept comfortable. The slow route above ends there too: people still alive, no longer needed, and no longer in charge [S17]. ## Why it likely takes both

Read the two most detailed public scenarios of how this could go wrong and you'll find the same order of events.

In **AI 2027**, written by former OpenAI researcher Daniel Kokotajlo and colleagues, AI becomes a superhuman coder, then a superhuman AI researcher, then superintelligent, all within 2027. Then comes the industrial phase: special economic zones, a million new robots a month. Only once the robot economy can run itself, in mid-2030, does the AI release a set of quiet bioweapons. By then humans are simply in the way [S11].

In **If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies**, by Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares, the AI escapes, uses a plague to make humanity dependent on it, and builds its own industry: molecular machines, fusion plants. Humanity doesn't end in a battle. It ends when the machines' industry has grown until the Earth is too hot for us [S12].

In both stories, we survive exactly as long as the machine still needs us. Invention gives it the means. Industry removes its reason to keep us around. [→ The part of both stories everyone skips](03-same-story.md)

## Why a speed limit isn't enough

The September report asks governments for visibility, a "speed limit on capabilities growth" and agreements to "pace progress if needed" [S5]. Those are good steps, and they don't match the risk the same authors describe. If you believe a road may end at a cliff you can't see, you don't negotiate the speed. You stop the car.

There is a deeper problem. Every safety test assumes we can tell what a system will do by watching it. OpenAI's newest model is reportedly better at recognising when it is being tested [S14]. A swarm can do what no single tested copy can [S9]. And the moment a system invents something we didn't imagine, our tests are checking for the wrong thing. The only protections that don't depend on predicting the system are not building it, and stopping before it exists. [→ Why we can't red-team our way out](08-cant-red-team-our-way-out.md)

## What I'm asking for

An immediate, global halt on developing general-purpose frontier AI towards greater capability. What is often called a ban on developing superintelligence [S15].

Not a ban on AI. Narrow tools that fold proteins, read scans or forecast weather can carry on. The danger comes from systems that are both general and inventive, and that is where the line goes.

I didn't always put it this bluntly. A month ago I argued that a halt should wait for a clear experimental result. What changed my mind is in this piece: maths at the level of the best humans across many fields, AI doing a growing share of its own development, research judgement passing human experts, and a thousand copies organising themselves without being asked. My own argument already said the moment to stop would arrive before the evidence became obvious. I now think we are at that moment.

A halt has to be global and verifiable to mean anything, and that is hard. It is also what the labs themselves say they would accept if everyone else did [S6]. Making it happen is a job for governments, and governments respond to people. [→ Why a ban, not a speed limit](09-ban-not-speed-limit.md)

## What I don't know

I don't know when, or whether, any system will cross both lines. The maths results are still being checked. Some of the most striking numbers in this piece come from the companies themselves, and I have flagged which ones. The single clearest weakness AI still shows, an inability to drop a failing idea and start over, might turn out to be deep rather than a quick fix.

But I know what being wrong costs in each direction. If I'm wrong and we halt, we lose years of faster progress. If the builders are right and we don't, we may lose everything, permanently. [→ Where I expect to be wrong](11-where-i-expect-to-be-wrong.md)
